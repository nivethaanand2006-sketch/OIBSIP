The Tribute

A story kept alive for twenty-five centuries

Icarus was never a god, a king, or a warrior — he was a prisoner's son, known to history for a single afternoon of flight. In Greek mythology he was the child of Daedalus, the master craftsman of Athens who had designed the great Labyrinth of Knossos for King Minos of Crete, a maze built to contain the monstrous Minotaur. When Minos discovered that Daedalus had helped Theseus escape that same Labyrinth, he had father and son locked away themselves, shut inside a tower — or by some tellings, the Labyrinth's own walls — with the sea on every side and no ship allowed near the shore.

Trapped by land and by water, Daedalus turned his genius toward the one route Minos could never guard: the open sky. He gathered feathers of every size, binding the small ones with thread and the larger ones with beeswax, curving each pair into the shape of a bird's wing. He tested his own set first, rising a little off the ground to be sure the design would hold, then fastened a matching pair to his son's shoulders. As he tied the straps, the old stories say his hands trembled and his eyes filled with tears — he knew exactly how fragile his invention was.

Daedalus gave Icarus a single instruction: fly the middle course. Too low, and the sea's spray would soak the feathers and drag him under; too high, and the sun's heat would loosen the wax that held everything together. For a while Icarus obeyed, following his father's path over the islands of the Aegean. But the sensation of flight overtook him — the freedom of it, after years shut behind stone walls, was intoxicating. He climbed higher, chasing the open sky, until the sun softened the wax past holding. Feather by feather, his wings came apart in the air, and he fell into the sea that would carry his name.

Daedalus, gliding on and hearing no answer to his calls, found only floating feathers where his son had been. He buried what the water gave back on a nearby island and named the surrounding sea the Icarian Sea in his son's memory — a name that particular stretch of water, near modern-day Ikaria, has carried ever since. Icarus never ruled a kingdom or won a battle, but his fall did something few myths achieve: it gave an entire civilization a shorthand for the exact moment ambition outruns caution, still spoken today as "flying too close to the sun."

The Myth, Step by Step

From the Labyrinth to the Icarian Sea

The story survives in several ancient versions — most fully in Ovid's Metamorphoses — but its arc has stayed remarkably consistent for over two thousand years. Tilt your cursor across each card below.

I.

Imprisonment at Knossos

After Theseus escapes the Labyrinth with the help of Ariadne's thread of string, King Minos suspects Daedalus gave away the maze's secret. He has Daedalus and young Icarus sealed away — in a tower by the sea in most accounts, or inside the Labyrinth itself in others.

II.

Daedalus invents the wings

With every ship and shoreline watched, Daedalus reasons that Minos "rules the land and sea, but not the air." He collects feathers, binding small ones with linen thread and larger ones with beeswax, and shapes two pairs of wings — one for himself, one for his son.

III.

The warning

Before takeoff, Daedalus fastens the wings to Icarus's shoulders and gives him careful instructions: keep to the middle height. Fly too near the sea and the damp will weigh the feathers down; fly too near the sun and its heat will melt the wax apart.

IV.

The flight from Crete

Father and son lift off the tower and head out over the Aegean, Daedalus leading and glancing back to check on his son. Below them, fishermen, shepherds, and ploughmen stop their work, certain they are watching gods pass overhead.

V.

The fall

Exhilarated by the height and freedom of flight, Icarus drifts away from his father's path and climbs toward the sun. The wax binding his feathers softens, then gives way entirely. He plunges into the sea near the island later called Icaria, and drowns.

VI.

A name that outlived him

Daedalus recovers his son's body and buries him on the nearest island. He names the surrounding waters the Icarian Sea in his memory — a stretch of the Aegean that still carries the name today, alongside the Greek island of Ikaria.
